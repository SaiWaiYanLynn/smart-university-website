Capability Survey of University Student Service Websites
What universities let a student do online, and what they do not

Project: Smart University Website | IT Project I and II (BC362916 + BC362914) | Instructor: Dr. Aftab Alam Janisar | 6 October 2026


1. Why We Did Not Choose One University
We are not building this system for a single institution, so we did not pick one university to study. We are building an open
reference implementation that any university can adopt. The backend, the agent layer, the APIs and the data model are the
product. A university that adopts it supplies its own regulation documents, its own student data, its own identity provider
and its own channel accounts, and changes the frontend and the database to match what it already runs. Nothing in the
design depends on one university's rules.
That choice creates one obligation. If we are not studying a client, we still have to show that the problem is real. This report
is how we show it. Instead of asking one university what it lacks, we measured what universities actually offer their students
today, across 17 institutions in Thailand and abroad, and recorded every answer so that a reader can check any row.


2. Method
What we did. On 5 and 6 October 2026 we reviewed the public websites of 17 universities, 12 in Thailand and 5
international. For each university we answered the same seven capability questions, using public pages only, including
registrar sites, library sites and official channel pages. We also reviewed published research on university chatbots, including
Thai journal papers, to see how far existing assistants go.
Why these seven questions. The questions are the capabilities our system provides. We are not judging whether a university
website is good in general. We are measuring whether a student can reach these services, see what happened to them, and do
any of it by conversation. A university can score well on the first four questions and still fail the last three, and that is the gap
we are investigating.
How to read a cross. A cross means the capability is not offered to students, or that we found no public evidence of it after a
targeted search of that university's own sites. Where a cross is backed by an explicit statement, the note says so, for example
a library that states its rooms are walk-in only. Since this survey measures what a student can discover and use from outside,
a capability we cannot find is a capability the student cannot find either.
Limits we accept. We did not log in to any student portal, so a university may offer more inside its portal than is visible to
us. We did not survey students, so this report measures what the systems do, not what students feel about them. Every row
can be rechecked from the sources in Section 6.

5. What the Results Show
Universities are well equipped on the basics, and we say so plainly. 13 of 17 let a student submit a request through a real
online form, 11 of 17 allow a room to be booked online, and 15 of 17 already run an assistant or an official chat channel.
Any claim that universities have no online services is easy to disprove, so we do not make it. Four are exceptions: KMUTNB
still takes transcript requests in person at the counter, Srinakharinwirot has no online route we could find, Bangkok
University's "online" route is an email with a photo of a bank transfer slip, and at STOU we could confirm enrolment services
but not document requests.
Visibility after submitting is rare. Only 3 of 17 show a student the state of a request without contacting staff, and all three
are international. None of the 12 Thai universities do. A Thai student who submits a request is told how many working days
to wait and then has to show up.
Conversation is already normal, but only for asking. 15 of 17 have a chat channel, and in Thailand it is almost always
LINE. Students already accept chat as a way to deal with their university. The channel is there and is not being used to do
anything.

   The gap, stated precisely. Across all 17 universities, none scored a tick on C5, C6 or C7. Not one assistant answers
   from the university's own regulation documents and shows the rule it came from. Not one completes a request or a
   booking inside the conversation and then reports its state. Not one offers these services from a single place. The three
   closest cases are partial: NUS takes a status letter request in chat but answers in four working days outside the chat,
   and the LINE bots at KMUTNB and Buriram Rajabhat run fixed scripts. Services exist, the chat channel exists, and
   nothing joins them.



6. Why the Assistants That Exist Do Not Close the Gap
The assistants we found are intent-based. A developer writes an intent such as "request transcript", gives it a set of example
phrasings, and the system learns to route a message into one of those buckets and run the flow attached to it. These systems
can perform real actions, so the limitation is not capability. The limitation is coverage: everything the assistant can handle
was written down in advance by a person, and anything outside that list reaches a fallback reply. Adding a service means
hand-building a new intent and a new flow.
Published work supports this. A 2025 review of university chatbots in higher education is framed around frequently asked
questions, and the chatbot literature describes the main weakness as limited ability to go beyond the data the system was
given. Thai universities have published several of these: an intent-based LINE service at KMUTNB, a chatbot covering eight
service areas at Buriram Rajabhat, and a LINE-based graduate service system reported in IJECE. Each is built for one
institution and one set of scripted intents.
What our project does differently. It retrieves from the university's published regulations, so it can answer a question
nobody scripted and show the rule behind the answer. It selects an action by reading what each tool does, rather than by
matching a trained intent, so new phrasings and combined requests still work. It completes the request, tracks its state, and
reports back on the same chat channel the student started on. And it is built to be adopted rather than built for one campus.
